# Greek theme and source map

This is a research index for future themed weeks. It does not define the lesson sequence or imply that all listed sources tell the same story.

| Theme | Strong core sources | Useful flavor pairings | Research caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cosmic origins and succession | Hesiod, *Theogony*; selected Homeric Hymns; later mythography | Fry, *Mythos* | Present Hesiod’s account as one influential genealogy, not the single Greek creation canon. |
| Gods, worship, and local identity | Homeric Hymns; Pausanias; Herodotus; Burkert; inscriptions and sanctuary objects | *Hades*, *Hades II* | Literary gods, local cult practice, and modern character designs answer different questions. |
| Prometheus, Pandora, and human life | Hesiod, *Theogony* and *Works and Days*; Aeschylus, *Prometheus Bound* (with authorship caveat) | Fry, *Mythos*; *Hades II* | Separate Hesiod’s poems and the tragedy; don’t blend the story into one sequence without marking the sources. |
| Persephone, Demeter, seasons, and the Underworld | *Homeric Hymn to Demeter*; Pausanias; relevant cult scholarship, including Foley and Burkert | Miller, *Circe* (adjacent Underworld material); *Hadestown*; *Hades* | Do not treat the hymn as a full ethnography of the Eleusinian Mysteries; ritual details may be limited or debated. |
| Heroes and heroic identity | *Iliad*; *Odyssey*; Pindar; Apollodorus; relevant tragedies | Fry, *Heroes*; Disney’s *Hercules*; Riordan | “Hero” differs by text, period, and context; keep figures such as Heracles and Achilles distinct. |
| The Labyrinth, Theseus, and Ariadne | Apollodorus; Plutarch where relevant; vase imagery; Pausanias | Saint, *Ariadne*; Riordan | Local and literary evidence may not support the familiar unified version; trace each episode to its witness. |
| Jason, Medea, and the Argonauts | Apollonius, *Argonautica*; Euripides, *Medea*; Pindar; Apollodorus | Fry, *Heroes*; Miller, *Circe*; Haynes | The Medea of the tragedy and of the epic are literary constructions in different works and contexts. |
| Troy: epic, tragedy, and aftermath | *Iliad*; surviving tragedy; fragments and testimonia of the Epic Cycle; Apollodorus; Ovid as Roman reception | Fry, *Troy*; Haynes, *A Thousand Ships*; Barker, *The Silence of the Girls* | The *Iliad* does not narrate the whole war. Identify where each episode survives and where evidence is fragmentary. |
| Fate, prophecy, kinship, and civic conflict | Sophocles’ Theban plays; Aeschylus’ *Oresteia*; Euripides | Haynes, *The Children of Jocasta* | Tragedy reshapes inherited stories for performance and its own audience. Don’t mistake dramatic choices for a single ancient doctrine. |
| Transformation, monsters, and the divine body | Hesiod; Homer; Pindar; Apollodorus; Ovid, *Metamorphoses* (Roman reception); vase evidence | Disney’s *Hercules*; *Hades*; *Hades II* | The same figure can have different bodies, origins, and roles across sources. Make the version explicit. |
| Myth across pantheons (future cross-cultural theme) | Select Greek sources above plus separately researched primary and scholarly sources for each additional culture | Selected modern works, clearly labeled | Compare a carefully defined question, not assumed universal archetypes. Do not claim that one tradition proves what another “really means.” |

## Good first pilot for source research

Before drafting lessons, one manageable pilot could be “Persephone and the Underworld,” because it supports:

- close reading of one substantial ancient poem;
- careful distinction between literary narrative and religious/cult evidence;
- comparison of later Greek and Roman retellings;
- vivid modern pairings in *Hadestown* and *Hades*;
- a clear demonstration of the core/flavor separation.

The pilot should first build a passage-level source packet, not a week of lesson copy: exact passage locators, edition/translation metadata, a short neutral summary, variant notes, and reviewer notes for any interpretive claims.
